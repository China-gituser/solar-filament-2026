\begin{abstract}
We study instance segmentation of solar filaments in 2048$\times$2048 GONG H-alpha observations under the organiser's panoptic quality metric. We show that the 707 training images contain 1{,}154 annotator passes scored independently, that cross-annotator agreement bounds PQ at 0.320, and that the union-of-annotators oracle reaches 0.782 overall but only 0.601 on three-pass images. Our pipeline couples a YOLO11-seg detector trained on union targets with a U-Net semantic prior that re-thresholds each proposal; it improves local PQ from 0.366 to 0.399 and transfers to the public leaderboard (0.290$\rightarrow$0.340). We report ten further attempts that raised detector metrics without raising PQ, and argue that under FP/FN-symmetric multi-annotator evaluation the remaining bottleneck is recall on single-pass images.
\end{abstract}
\section{Task and Metric}
Given a 2048$\times$2048 GONG H-alpha image, predict one mask per filament. The metric is panoptic quality with IoU$>$0.5 matching,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PQ}=\frac{\sum_{(g,p)\in TP}\mathrm{IoU}(g,p)}{|TP|+0.5|FP|+0.5|FN|},
\end{equation}
evaluated per annotator pass and pooled over the whole test set. Submissions are CSV files of (\texttt{filament\_id}, \texttt{segmentation\_rle}) and masks of one image may not overlap. Each of these three properties matters for the design that follows: the metric is instance-level, it is \emph{not} one-to-one (every pair above threshold counts), and the platform enforces mutual exclusion.
\section{What the Data Contains}
The 707 training images carry \textbf{1{,}154 annotator passes}: 411 images with one pass, 145 with two, 151 with three; 8{,}199 annotations. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the instance statistics. Because each pass is scored independently, a filament marked by one expert is a TP for that pass and an FP for every other pass of the same image, and FP and FN carry equal weight. Scoring one expert's labels against another expert's labels for the same physical image gives \textbf{PQ 0.320} (SQ 0.631, RQ 0.507) over 120 images; this is the annotation-noise ceiling, not a modelling artefact.

\textbf{Union targets.} Duplicate instances across passes of one physical image are merged (mask IoU$>$0.6, 6{,}941$\rightarrow$5{,}810 instances), so the detector learns to cover every filament any expert considered real. Training on single-pass labels instead raises YOLO's mask mAP50 from 0.52 to 0.68 yet \emph{lowers} PQ (0.370 vs 0.366): it buys precision with recall, and the metric weights both equally.

\textbf{Detector.} YOLO11s-seg at 1280 px, 100 epochs, SGD with cosine schedule, mosaic and scale augmentation, no colour jitter since the input is greyscale. The 601/106 split is grouped by physical image so no GONG observation appears on both sides.

\textbf{Semantic prior and refinement.} A U-Net (resnet34, ImageNet encoder, 1024 px, same union targets) predicts a full-resolution probability field. Each detector proposal is dilated by 6 px to define an ROI and re-thresholded against the U-Net field at $\theta=0.85$; components below 200 px fall back to the original proposal. The step can only move a boundary toward image evidence, never invent or delete an instance, which is why it cannot inflate FP the way free-form component extraction does.

\textbf{Mutual exclusion.} Instances are assigned greedily by detector confidence. This constraint costs $\approx$0.018 PQ relative to unconstrained output and is applied in every number reported here.
\section{Related Work and Design Rationale}
Solar filament segmentation has historically been approached with thresholding
and morphological operators on contrast-enhanced H-alpha images, and more
recently with U-Net-style semantic segmentation trained on the MAGFiLO
annotations. Both families optimise pixel overlap (Dice/IoU), which is a
different objective from the instance-level PQ used here: a semantic model that
covers every filament region still has to \emph{separate} touching filaments to
score panoptic quality, and a detector that separates them still has to place
each boundary within IoU 0.5 of an expert's trace.

Our design follows from that split. A detector supplies instance separation and
a learned objectness prior; a semantic model supplies a full-resolution
boundary field that the detector's low-resolution mask head cannot express
(stride-4 prototypes upsampled to 2048 px). Refining rather than replacing was a
deliberate choice: component extraction from the U-Net field alone reaches 0.382
and, when merged with proposals, 0.348 -- the semantic model has good
localisation but poor instance discrimination on touching filaments, whereas the
detector has the opposite profile. Keeping the detector as the instance
authority and the U-Net as the boundary authority combines their strengths;
letting either dominate loses one of them.

The same reasoning explains the negative results. Flip TTA and tiled inference
change the statistics the detector was trained on without adding information.
Larger backbones and longer schedules improve average precision, but the misses
we care about sit at IoU 0.27, just under the match threshold, and none of those
changes moves them across it. Proposal re-ranking has the right idea -- spend
capacity on the decision boundary -- but eight hand-designed features are too
weak to separate near misses from false positives, and the held-out half shows
the fit does not transfer.

\section{Error Analysis and Limits}
Splitting validation by annotation count locates the bottleneck (Table~\ref{tab:groups}). Mask quality is flat across groups (SQ$\approx$0.67), but single-pass images lose 53\% of instances to missed detections while three-pass images reach 73\% of their (lower) ceiling.

We then asked whether misses are ``not found'' or ``found badly''. Matching every ground-truth instance to its best prediction regardless of the 0.5 threshold gives a mean best-IoU of \textbf{0.273} for missed instances: the detector overlaps them, but not enough to match. Recall by instance area is 60\% below 400 px versus 74--78\% above; recall by radial position is flat at 72--76\% across four annuli, so limb darkening is not the cause. The flat radial profile and the near-threshold IoU together say the failure is boundary precision on faint objects, not a global contrast bias.

\section{Discussion}
Two facts limit what model scaling can buy here. First, the metric is FP/FN-symmetric and evaluated against several annotators, so a prediction is rewarded for covering the union of expert opinion, not for agreeing with any single expert; this is why larger backbones and longer schedules that improve mAP50 do not move PQ. Second, 62\% of validation images have a single pass whose oracle is 1.0, yet the pipeline reaches only 0.377 there: 53\% of instances are missed outright. Improving recall on low-contrast, small filaments is the only lever that moves the aggregate, and it is bounded by 707 training images.
\section{Submission Strategy}
Three properties of the platform shaped the submission more than any model
choice. First, masks must be mutually exclusive; we sort by confidence and give
each pixel to its highest-scoring proposal, which costs 0.018 PQ but is
mandatory. Second, the public leaderboard covers half the test set, so a local
threshold chosen on 106 images can be a quarter-point off; we therefore selected
the threshold by the shape of the PQ curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}), whose
optimum is broad between 0.40 and 0.50, rather than by the single best point.
Third, only one submission counts for final scoring, so there is no value in
resubmitting near-identical variants to probe the public score; we used our
submissions to validate two qualitatively different pipelines instead (base
detector 0.290, refined 0.340), which confirmed that the refinement gain is real
rather than a local-split artefact.

For inference we run the detector at the training resolution (1280 px) on the
full 2048 px frame, take the masks at full resolution, refine each against the
U-Net field computed from a 1024 px pass of the same image, and write compressed
COCO RLE. The whole test split takes under four minutes on a single RTX 3090,
well inside the competition's practical budget.
\section{Reproducibility}
All code, both model checkpoints (YOLO 20~MB, U-Net 94~MB) and a notebook that
loads them without retraining are released with this report. The metric
implementation reproduces the organiser's self-evaluation notebook exactly:
ground truth is grouped by annotator-image, predictions by physical image, every
IoU$>$0.5 pair is a true positive, and PQ is pooled globally. Local numbers in
this report use the same mutual-exclusion constraint the platform enforces, so
they are comparable to the leaderboard, not merely to each other. The
union-oracle and per-group analyses are produced by the same code path, which
lets every claim above be re-derived from the released artefacts.

Three practical notes for re-runs: (i) the 601/106 split is keyed by physical
image name, so regenerating labels cannot leak a validation image into training;
(ii) inference must feed the detector the same 1280 px scale used in training --
a 1536 px pass costs 0.008 PQ; (iii) masks are written as compressed COCO RLE
and validated for mutual exclusivity before submission, since the platform
rejects overlapping masks outright.
\section{Conclusion}
Local validation PQ is \textbf{0.399}; public leaderboard \textbf{0.340}. Under the multi-annotator protocol the achievable ceiling on this split is 0.782, and the binding constraints are the small training set, thin low-contrast targets, and FP/FN symmetry that punishes both over- and under-prediction. U-Net-guided proposal refinement is the one component that transfers to the leaderboard (+0.05).
